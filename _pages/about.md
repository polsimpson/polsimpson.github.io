## Working Papers

<div class="paper">
  <h3><a href="/files/JalalSimpson_floods.pdf">The Economic Incidence of Natural Disasters</a> <span class="badge">Job Market Paper</span></h3>
  <p class="coauthors">with <a href="https://amenjalal.com">Amen Jalal</a></p>
  <div class="actions">
    <details>
      <summary>Abstract</summary>
      <div class="content">Policymakers and researchers often view the costs of natural disasters as local to the areas they physically strike. We show that due to spatial linkages across markets, the economic incidence of a disaster extends well beyond its physical footprint. We study Pakistan’s large-scale 2022 floods, combining original panel surveys of 5,100 rural households with satellite flood maps and a recentred instrument based on a hydrological model. We find that moving a household from the 10th to the 90th percentile of flooding within 15km of its home lowers its real expenditure by 10%, of which nearly two-thirds is due to flooding more than 5km away from home. The same change in flooding reduces the share of men in wage employment by 9 percentage points (pp), raises food prices by 8% and asset sales by 3 pp, and lowers credit access by 5 pp. These effects are driven almost entirely by flooding more than 5km away. We quantify the economic incidence of flooding in general equilibrium by building a spatial model of trade and commuting. Although two in five locations experienced almost no flooding, the model implies that welfare fell everywhere, and that 73% of the average loss was caused by flooding elsewhere. Moreover, targeting aid on predicted losses rather than physical exposure achieves the same welfare at 18% lower cost. Thus, ignoring the divergence between economic incidence and physical exposure leads us to underestimate disaster costs and misallocate aid, particularly for larger disasters and more integrated economies.</div>

<div class="paper">
  <h3><a href="https://poid.lse.ac.uk/textonly/publications/downloads/poidwp143.pdf">Ray of Hope? China and the Rise of Solar Energy</a></h3>
  <p class="coauthors">with <a href="https://ignaciobanares.com">Ignacio Bañares Sánchez</a>, <a href="https://www.robinburgess.com">Robin Burgess</a>, Dávid László, <a href="https://www.johnvanreenen.com">John Van Reenen</a> and <a href="https://www.yifanwang.net">Yifan Wang</a></p>
  <p class="status">Revise and Resubmit, <strong>The Quarterly Journal of Economics</strong></p>
  <div class="actions">
    <details>
      <summary>Abstract</summary>
      <div class="content">Do industrial policies that promote clean energy offer a "ray of hope", increasing a country's growth and welfare, whilst simultaneously reducing carbon emissions? We study the impact of Chinese solar subsidies whose implementation by city-regions went alongside massive expansion of the sector and a dramatic fall in global solar prices. We construct new city and firm panel data on solar policies, patenting and output. Using synthetic-difference-in-differences 2004-2020, we find production and innovation subsidies were more effective than demand-side (installation) subsidies in generating large and persistent increases in local innovation, net entry, production and exports. Demand policies did, however, reduce local pollution. To examine aggregate effects, we build and structurally estimate a quantitative spatial model with endogenous innovation and heterogeneous productivity across firms and cities, which accounts for business stealing and knowledge spillovers. Counterfactual analysis shows that: (i) local effects remain substantial at the macro level explaining 40%–50% of the aggregate changes in solar innovation, prices and revenues; (ii) social benefits to Chinese citizens exceed subsidy costs by 65% (and double this when environmental benefits are included); and (iii) although all subsidy types increase welfare, innovation subsidies are the most cost-effective.</div>
    </details>

<div class="paper">
  <h3><a href="https://emilianorinaldi.com/files/Rinaldi_JMP.pdf">Rebalancing Water? Evidence from Day Zero in Cape Town</a></h3>
  <p class="coauthors">with <a href="https://emilianorinaldi.com">Emiliano Rinaldi</a></p>
  <div class="actions">
    <details>
      <summary>Abstract</summary>
      <div class="content">During a drought, a city must allocate a large reduction in water between households and the firms that employ them. A single price allocates water efficiently when each user's willingness to pay equals the social marginal value of their water consumption, which may fail in the presence of externalities or other distortions. We study Cape Town's 2016-2018 drought, when the city increased water prices sharply for households and firms. Using the universe of water meter and tax data, we find that water price increases led to large and lasting reductions in employment in exposed sectors, driven by establishment closure. Wages in these sectors did not fall, even for new hires, and exit was concentrated among small establishments, consistent with downward wage rigidity and credit constraints. Such distortions create a wedge, with the social value of water to firms rising above their willingness to pay for it. In contrast, households cut consumption sharply but remained above health and sanitation thresholds. To quantify the social cost of reductions in firm water use, we build and estimate a dynamic model of the city with these distortions and costly labour reallocation. Using the model, we find that placing a larger conservation burden on households could have reduced the drought's welfare cost by 52 percent. When policymakers cannot address underlying frictions directly, resource pricing can be used as a second best policy to reduce the welfare cost of temporary shortages.</div>

<div class="paper">
  <h3>Heat Insurance at Work</h3>
  <p class="coauthors">with <a href="https://amenjalal.com">Amen Jalal</a>, <a href="https://www.ashleypople.com">Ashley Pople</a>, <a href="https://kartiksrivastava.com">Kartik Srivastava</a>, <a href="https://sites.google.com/view/eddyzou/home">Eddy Zou</a> and <a href="https://orianabandiera.github.io">Oriana Bandiera</a></p>
  <div class="actions">
    <details>
      <summary>Abstract</summary>
      <div class="content">Heatwaves, intensified by climate change, hit the poorest the hardest. Many are exposed to dangerous temperatures through outdoor or factory-based work, and have limited access to adaptive resources. How can social protection evolve to address the growing losses caused by extreme heat? We conduct a randomized controlled trial among low-income workers in Delhi, India, comparing the effects of temperature-indexed heat alerts, anticipatory daily wage payments delivered before heat events, and same-day daily wage payments delivered during heatwaves. We measure impacts on labour supply, adaptive behaviours, health, and financial wellbeing. The design separately identifies the value of information, the value of cash, and the value of acting ahead of heatwaves.</div>

<div class="paper">
  <h3>The Fundamental Need for Shelter: Evidence from the World's Largest Home Reconstruction Program</h3>
  <p class="coauthors">with <a href="https://amenjalal.com">Amen Jalal</a> and <a href="https://canishknaik.github.io">Canishk Naik</a></p>
  <div class="actions">
    <details>
      <summary>Abstract</summary>
      <div class="content">Psychologists have long argued that human needs are met in order of priority: until basic needs such as shelter are secured, attention and effort remain focused on them, delaying higher-order pursuits. This idea has implications for a range of economic outcomes — shaping, for instance, how much background risk a household bears and, thus, how willing it is to make high-risk, high-reward investments in the future. Despite its influence, the idea that needs follow a hierarchy has rarely been tested using credible causal evidence on economic behaviour. We provide such a test using the reconstruction of flood-destroyed homes in Sindh, Pakistan. Exploiting staggered disbursements of home-reconstruction grants, we compare otherwise-similar households that received funding just before and just after a program expansion. Combining administrative records with a survey of 6,000 households, we estimate the effects of home security on risk-taking, migration, health, schooling, and productive investment.</div>
